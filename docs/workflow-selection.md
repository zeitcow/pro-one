# Workflow Selection

Pro-One should choose supported legal workflows carefully.

A legal workflow should not be added only because it is common, interesting, or technically possible. It should be useful, source-grounded, limited, testable, and safe enough for the project to support responsibly.

## Purpose

This document explains how Pro-One should decide which workflows to build, support, delay, narrow, or reject.

A workflow means a structured legal-information task, such as:

- helping a user understand a legal notice
- explaining common steps in a process
- collecting facts for a form
- drafting a plain-language letter
- summarizing source-grounded legal information
- pointing a user to official resources
- helping a small business understand a basic compliance issue

A workflow does not mean open-ended legal advice.

## Core selection standard

A workflow should be considered only when it satisfies most of these criteria:

- it serves a real access-to-justice need
- it can be explained in plain language
- it can be grounded in reliable sources
- it has a clear jurisdiction or source boundary
- it does not require guessing legal deadlines
- it can be evaluated with examples
- it can warn users about limits
- it does not require unnecessary sensitive data
- it can be narrowed when the risk is too high

A workflow that cannot meet these criteria should be delayed or redesigned.

## Preferred early workflows

Early Pro-One workflows should be narrow, practical, and low-risk.

Good early candidates include:

- explaining common legal terms in plain language
- helping users organize facts before seeking legal help
- summarizing official self-help resources
- helping users identify possible next questions
- generating non-filing drafts with clear disclaimers
- helping small businesses understand basic document categories
- pointing users to court, agency, or legal aid resources

These workflows are useful without pretending to replace a lawyer.

## Workflows that require caution

Some workflows may be useful but require extra review.

Examples include:

- drafting demand letters
- helping with landlord-tenant disputes
- helping with employment disputes
- helping users understand court notices
- helping small businesses review basic contract language
- explaining administrative agency processes
- helping users prepare for legal aid intake
- helping users compare official process steps

These workflows may be appropriate if they are narrow, source-grounded, and clear about limits.

## High-risk workflows

High-risk workflows should not be added without strong safeguards, legal-source review, evaluation, and clear limits.

Examples include workflows involving:

- court deadlines
- appeals
- defaults
- eviction
- foreclosure
- bankruptcy
- criminal charges
- immigration status
- family law
- child custody
- domestic violence
- emergency relief
- medical facts
- large financial consequences
- loss of liberty, housing, custody, immigration status, or major rights

A high-risk workflow may need to be rejected, narrowed, or limited to routing users to official resources and qualified help.

## Selection questions

Before adding a workflow, ask:

- Who is the user?
- What problem are they trying to solve?
- What outcome are they expecting?
- What could go wrong if the output is wrong?
- What sources support the workflow?
- What jurisdiction does the workflow apply to?
- What facts are required?
- What facts should not be collected?
- What limits must be shown to the user?
- How can the workflow be evaluated?
- When should the workflow refuse or redirect?

If these questions cannot be answered, the workflow is not ready.

## User need

A workflow should solve a real user problem.

The project should prioritize workflows that help:

- self-represented individuals understand legal processes
- individuals organize information before seeking help
- individuals find official resources
- small businesses understand basic legal-document needs
- small businesses prepare questions for a lawyer
- users avoid confusion caused by legal language

The project should avoid workflows that are mostly impressive demos but do not help users make safer, clearer decisions.

## Source availability

A workflow should have reliable sources.

Preferred sources include:

- official court websites
- official agency websites
- statutes
- rules
- forms
- instructions
- public legal aid resources
- court self-help materials
- recognized secondary sources where appropriate

If reliable sources are not available, the workflow should not provide confident legal information.

## Jurisdiction clarity

Legal information depends heavily on jurisdiction.

A workflow should identify:

- country
- state
- locality, when relevant
- court or agency, when relevant
- source date, when relevant

If jurisdiction cannot be determined, the workflow should explain the limitation and avoid specific legal conclusions.

## Scope control

A supported workflow should have a clear scope.

It should define:

- what the workflow does
- what the workflow does not do
- which users it is for
- which sources it relies on
- which jurisdictions it supports
- which risks require refusal or redirection

A workflow with unclear scope should not be launched.

## Legal advice boundary

Pro-One should provide legal information, not legal advice.

A workflow should avoid:

- telling a user what they should do in a specific legal matter
- predicting the outcome of a case
- making unsupported legal conclusions
- selecting a legal strategy for the user
- calculating deadlines without reliable source support
- pretending that AI output is a lawyer's judgment

The workflow may help users understand options, organize facts, find sources, and prepare questions.

## Privacy impact

A workflow should collect only the information needed for the task.

Before selecting a workflow, ask:

- Does the workflow require sensitive facts?
- Can the task work with less information?
- Does the workflow need documents?
- Could uploaded documents contain private legal, financial, family, housing, employment, immigration, or business information?
- Can the system avoid storing the information?
- Can the user be warned before sharing sensitive information?

Privacy risk should affect workflow priority.

## Evaluation feasibility

A workflow should be testable.

Before treating a workflow as supported, the project should define:

- sample user questions
- expected behavior
- refusal examples
- source-grounded answer examples
- citation expectations
- known limitations
- edge cases
- unsafe answer examples

A workflow that cannot be evaluated should not be treated as reliable.

## Failure modes

Each workflow should identify likely failure modes.

Examples include:

- wrong jurisdiction
- outdated source
- missing deadline
- invented citation
- unsupported legal conclusion
- overconfident answer
- user misunderstanding a limitation
- unsafe handling of uploaded documents
- failure to recommend qualified help when needed

The workflow should include safeguards for likely failures.

## Workflow readiness levels

Pro-One may use readiness levels to describe workflow maturity.

### Proposed

The workflow is an idea. It has not been reviewed.

### Researched

The workflow has identified users, sources, risks, and limits.

### Designed

The workflow has a written scope, source plan, safety plan, and evaluation plan.

### Tested

The workflow has sample questions, expected answers, refusal cases, and evaluation examples.

### Supported

The workflow is narrow, source-grounded, reviewed, tested, and documented.

A workflow should not be called supported until it has passed review.

## Approval criteria

A workflow may be approved for build work when:

- the user need is clear
- the workflow scope is narrow
- reliable sources are available
- jurisdiction limits are defined
- privacy risks are manageable
- legal advice boundaries are clear
- failure modes are documented
- evaluation examples can be created
- the workflow fits Pro-One's mission

Approval does not mean the workflow is ready for public use.

## Rejection or delay criteria

A workflow should be rejected, delayed, or narrowed when:

- source grounding is not possible
- jurisdiction is unclear
- legal harm from error is too high
- the workflow depends on guessing deadlines
- the workflow requires too much sensitive information
- evaluation is not feasible
- the workflow would imply legal advice
- the project cannot explain limitations clearly
- the workflow does not fit the mission

Saying no to a risky workflow protects users.

## Small business workflows

Small business workflows may be appropriate when they are practical and limited.

Examples include:

- explaining common contract sections
- helping organize facts for a lawyer
- identifying common compliance topics
- drafting plain-language business letters
- summarizing official agency guidance
- helping prepare questions before signing a document

Small business workflows should not replace legal review for important contracts, regulatory obligations, employment matters, tax issues, or disputes with major consequences.

## Self-represented user workflows

Self-represented user workflows should focus on clarity and safety.

Good goals include:

- explaining terms
- organizing facts
- identifying official resources
- explaining common process steps
- preparing questions
- helping users understand what information they may need

The project should be especially careful where a user could miss a deadline, waive a right, lose housing, lose custody, lose status, or suffer major financial harm.

## Human help and escalation

A workflow should identify when a user may need qualified help.

Examples include:

- urgent deadlines
- served court papers
- threats of eviction
- domestic violence
- criminal charges
- immigration consequences
- child custody disputes
- major financial exposure
- unclear jurisdiction
- facts that could materially change legal options

When the risk is high, the workflow should route users to official resources or qualified legal help rather than generating confident legal answers.

## Documentation requirement

Each supported workflow should eventually have a workflow document covering:

- purpose
- intended users
- supported jurisdictions
- sources
- inputs
- outputs
- limits
- refusal rules
- privacy considerations
- evaluation examples
- known risks
- review history

Until this exists, the workflow should be treated as planned or experimental.

## Guiding rule

Pro-One should build workflows that are useful because they are careful.

A smaller, source-grounded workflow is better than a broad workflow that gives users false confidence.
